# Data protection impact assessment (DPIA)

> An assessment of the impact of the NovaTrail platform's data processing on the rights and freedoms of learners — children above all. It is required by GDPR Art. 35 and the ICO's code, standard 2. Its steps and tables follow the ICO's template, "DPIA suggested process and template", whose steps 1–7 are its sections from the need for a DPIA to the sign-off, and the contents are those of Art. 35(7)(a)–(d).
>
> This is an internal record of the platform, not a text shown to people, and it is in English, like the other documents of `docs/`: a Claude Code session reads them. The repository is public, so the DPIA is published, as the ICO advises: "it is good practice to publish your DPIA". It holds no security details that would help an attack. It is given as it is, with its date and version, to the ICO (Art. 36(3)(e)) and to an AI provider at a review of the measures; the Russian text of the versions before 14 is in the git history. The platform has no lawyer: Claude writes the analysis, and the author accepts the measures and the residual risks.
>
> The sources were read on 2026-11-09, and the laws of Connecticut, Colorado and Montana on 2026-11-13, as the list of sources below says. The ICO's pages are read from Wayback Machine copies, because the site answers 403. They are marked "under review" after the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025.

## 2. The need for a DPIA

- **The ICO's code, std 2:** "Undertake a DPIA to assess and mitigate risks to the rights and freedoms of children who are likely to access your service, which arise from your data processing. Take into account differing ages, capacities and development needs and ensure that your DPIA builds in compliance with this code", and "if you offer an online service likely to be accessed by children, you must do a DPIA". The code applies also to a service from the USA that is open to children of the United Kingdom.
- **GDPR Art. 35(1)** — an assessment where processing, "in particular using new technologies", "is likely to result in a high risk".
- **The ICO's list** ("When do we need to do a DPIA?"). Of its items, these apply to the platform:
  - "Targeting of children or other vulnerable individuals: … if you intend to offer online services directly to children";
  - "Innovative technology: … (including AI)";
  - "Biometrics: any processing of biometric data" — the writing history in measurement samples from adults.
- **WP248 rev.01.** Of the nine criteria, three match: "Data concerning vulnerable data subjects" — "Vulnerable data subjects may include children", "Innovative use or applying new technological or organisational solutions" and "Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature" — essays and what they will disclose. And "a processing meeting two criteria would require a DPIA".
- **When.** Before the first child, wherever they live, and before the first learner from the EU or the United Kingdom, if that learner comes earlier. The ICO: "You must complete your DPIA before the service is launched, and ensure the outcomes can influence your design".

## 3. Describe the processing

### 3.1. Nature

- **Collection.**
  - The account owner signs in through the Google window and creates the learners' profiles themselves. A child does not create an account.
  - What is collected about a child: a pseudonym, an avatar from a set, the age category, the country, the interface language and the presentation style.
  - The learner's work stays on the device. What goes for checking is a crop or a picture of the work. While the storage of a profile's works is on, the server keeps each work as the device holds it, a stored work, and reads none.
  - On a task from paper the photo of a page goes in one request, to find its tasks — to a later model of the same provider only when the one before found none —, again with the learner's request about which tasks to take and the frames on the page, when the learner asks the tutor so, and the photo, the crops and the work stay in the profile's history on the device, never on the server.
  - Child consent — by a signed form, which the operator checks.
- **Use.**
  - The AI reads the work, and a program gives the verdict, but on a task from paper, which has no key and which a model judges. There the AI finds the tasks on the page, reads the statements of a crop as printed only on the learner's Recognise, which shows the learner the text, and at each press of Check judges the work of the frame's tasks — one, or up to 20 — in one call, the Check: it gets the task's crop as printed, the work with its strokes in sight, the crop of the instruction above the frame when there is one, the learner's age category and the attempt's history — the verdicts of the Checks before, the rungs shown and the tutor's words before —, and the strokes only as drawn in the work, never their points or the writing history, which several US states' laws count as biometric data, as the privacy document names them. It gives back each answer's verdict, the place of a mistake, its own solution and answer, hidden, the tutor's words and a ladder of hints, whose words a program checks for a leak of the hidden answer and for their form (risk 23); "explain differently" asks again from the crop as printed and the hidden solution, without the work. `api` keeps the reading of Recognise, the hidden solution and answer, the ladder, the boxes of the errors and the verdicts while the attempt is open, and none of them goes to the device; the verdict is the model's (risk 21), and the attempt gives no evidence.
  - The tutor chooses the words of a hint, and after a correct answer, praise for the step and a question about which technique helped.
  - The learner model and the engine choose the next task, with a reason code for the adult.
  - The adult sees the profile's progress.
- **Storage** — the database of `api`, the device, the sign-in service and the logs, for the periods of the retention policy (П-9).
- **Disclosure** — only to processors under an agreement, those of the table of processors below. The data are not sold or passed to anyone else.
- **Deletion and export** — in one step, by the account owner.

### 3.2. Scope

- **Kinds of data** — those `docs/privacy.md` lists. Special categories (Art. 9):
  - what an essay will disclose about the learner's health;
  - the writing history in measurement samples from adults — it is treated as biometrics. The writing history of a stored work is not, by Claude's reading: it is treated as biometrics in samples that go to others, whose purpose cannot be held, while on the platform's server nobody reads it and it serves no identification, so there it is a learner's personal information, kept only under the consent and out of any agent's reach.
- **What there is not:** a date of birth, the child's name, geolocation — a photo from paper goes and stays without the metadata of its file —, children's voice (О-6), communication between users, and advertising. The photo of a page from paper is in the first slice, for every profile.
- **How much.** Until the beta — the families of the closed beta. The scale grows with the public launch, and then the DPIA is reviewed.
- **Retention periods** — the retention policy of `docs/privacy.md`, П-9. The texts for adults and for the family write the policy.
- **Geography.** Learners from any country. The data lie in the USA, in `us-east4` and `aws-us-east-1`.

### 3.3. Context

- **Age.** The platform is for any age, and the service is open to children directly. There are four age categories, and what depends on the category is the AI route, the provider, voice and modules; a task from paper is open to every category.
- **Age and consent** — a parent's signed form, and by the public launch a card transaction as well. A teenager above the age of digital consent gives consent themselves, and the owner declares being an adult.
- **Parental controls.** A child learns under an adult's account. The section for adults is locked by a PIN. The adult sees progress — attempts, rubric marks, repeating traps — and the reason codes of the engine's choice. There is no real-time monitoring of the child and no tracking of their location.
- **Public concerns** — AI near children: what the model will say to a child, what the provider will do with their work, emotion inference. They are examined as risks below.
- **The family's expectations** are set by the promises in plain words and by the texts for adults and for the family.

### 3.4. Purposes and benefits

- **Purpose** — adaptive learning: to check a learner's work, to hint a step, to choose the next task by the learner model, and to check the tasks of the learner's own page, their homework, with hints.
- **Benefit for the child.** Checking on paper and on the canvas without a "red pen": "Not yet" instead of "incorrect", and for children — without timers and competitions, without advertising and dark patterns.
- **Commercial interest.** The family's subscription (О-14). Data are not sold, and there is no advertising.
- **Benefit for the family.** The adult sees the profile's progress and the reason for each choice of a task, and does not have to check the work themselves.
- **Benefit for others and the public.** The platform, its formats and the official modules' content are open under Apache-2.0: anyone may run a copy of their own, and the module format is open to any author. The published eval-set samples, from adults who agreed to it, let anyone check how a model reads handwriting.

## 5. Necessity and proportionality

### 5.1. Lawful basis and principles

- **Lawful basis.** An adult's own data — contract, Art. 6(1)(b). The data of a learner under 18 — consent, Art. 6(1)(a): below the age of digital consent, the parent's, under Art. 8, and under 13, also verifiable under COPPA.
- **Data minimisation:**
  - a pseudonym, not a name;
  - a category, not a date of birth;
  - a crop of the work on the device, not the whole sheet; on a task from paper the whole page goes in one request, to up to three models of one provider in turn, the next only when the one before did not do, and only its task frames come back, and the photo, the crops and the work stay on the device; the Check gets the crops of the task, of its work and of its instruction, the age category and the attempt's history, the strokes only as drawn, never their points or the writing history;
  - the server does not store the text of an essay, and the device keeps an essay only until the end of its attempt;
  - stored works on the server — only by choice, and storage is off by default.
- **Accuracy and bias:**
  - the verdict is given by a program, not by AI, but on a task from paper;
  - an observation is joined from two readers. The reading mode `single`, one reader with nothing to join, gives this measure up for every learner, a child too, and at every rung: the author's decisions (risk 20);
  - in doubt — a request to rewrite, not an "error";
  - on a task from paper a model judges the work, with no key: the Check, by the cascade of one provider, the next model only when the one before gave a `low` confidence, an `unreadable` answer or an answer off its schema; an unsure "Not yet" of the last model is shown, and the tutor's words say it more softly; "Couldn't check this one" is left only for a Check that read nothing of a solution; on a frame of several tasks each task gets its own verdict; the models are admitted by the author's word with no threshold of accuracy, and the eval set of verdicts measures false "Not yet" and false "Correct" apart before that word ends (risk 21);
  - a model is admitted only after eval. In the mode `single` the pair's second reader reads alone by the pair's entry, which no eval admitted alone. The author's word admits a model at the rung "author" without an eval while the author alone learns; it holds until 2027-01-17, and before the first code of `api invite` to another adult or the first child, each entry the word admitted passes an eval or leaves the list, or the author gives a new word (run/DEBT.md, Д265).
- **Explaining AI.** The learner is told that they are talking with AI, not with a person: in the adult's Help, on the tutor's panel of Home and on the page on the tutor; how it holds for a child is one of the conditions before the first child. The learner sees what was read.
- **Retention periods** — the retention policy.
- **Rights** — export and deletion in one step. What remains after deletion is a minimal record of consent, for 5 years.
- **Processors** — under the agreements of the table of processors above.

### 5.2. Proportionality of consent and age verification

The ICO: "A data protection impact assessment should help you to decide what steps you need to take to verify age and parental responsibility".
- **A parent's signed form.** A child's work goes to an AI provider, and an essay may disclose something personal. So the method is stronger than "email plus". But it requires no document of the child and no data of a verification service. By the public launch a card transaction is added: the more families there are, the less the operator can know them itself.
- **An adult — by declaration** (П-6). Sign-in through the Google window says nothing about age, and checking an adult's document is more data than the risk justifies. The cost of an error is a child with an adult's rights; against it stand the measures against risk 5.
- **The learner's age** is named by the owner, and the platform stores only the category. There is no separate check of the child's age: the parent who signed the form answers for it.

### 5.4. Benefits against risks

Connecticut, Colorado and Montana ask to weigh the benefits of the processing, to the operator, the learner, others and the public, against the risks to the learner as the measures leave them, and to take into account de-identified data, the learners' reasonable expectations, the context and the relationship, by the assessments of the three states below. Claude's weighing; the author accepts it in the sign-off below:
- **The benefits are the service itself.** The processing serves only learning: the check of a work, a hint, the choice of the next task. Without the learner model and the AI's reading of a work there is no adaptive learning, which is what the family came for.
- **The risks after the measures.** None is high. Medium: risks 2, 3, 5, 11, 12 and 17–19, risk 21 until the eval set of verdicts measures it, and risks 4, 20 and 21 under 13 (the table of measures and the sign-off below). The medium risks come from what a child writes or tells (2, 3), from what a model says (4), misreads (20) or misjudges (21), and from the limits of consent and of the texts (5, 11, 12, 17–19). None comes from a use of the data beyond learning: the data are not sold, not used for advertising or for others' purposes, and the AI providers do not keep or train on a child's work (measure 1).
- **De-identified data.** The platform keeps none in the laws' sense: such data cannot "reasonably be used to infer information about … an identified or identifiable individual" (CGS §42-515(16)), while every record of `api` belongs to a profile, so that the owner can export and delete it. Instead, the data identify nobody directly: a pseudonym and not a name, a category and not a date of birth, logs without direct identifiers, and a crop of the work and not the whole sheet, but for the page of a task from paper, which goes once, for its task frames alone.
- **Expectations, context and relationship.** The adult of the account chose the platform for the learner, gave the consent, and sees the progress. The child learns in the adult's account, and the texts for the family and for the child say what is kept and who sees it. What the platform does not do follows the promises in plain words: the data go only to processors, but for a letter a family writes to the operator's Gmail (risk 18) and a report to the authorities at a threat to a child (measure 3), and nothing a child does is public.
- **The outcome.** The benefits outweigh the residual risks: they serve the learner, and every risk but risk 20 has a measure: against a misreading of one reader there is none, the reading mode `single` gives the second reader up (measure 20). A medium residual risk either is to fall by a term — the conditions before the first child, and the open questions and the measurement named in the table of measures — or the author accepted it as it is, in the sign-off below: risks 2, 3 and 19, and risks 20 and 21 under 13.

## 6. Risks and measures

**The scale is the ICO template's.**
- Likelihood of harm — "Remote, possible or probable": remote, possible, probable.
- Severity of harm — "Minimal, significant or severe": minimal, significant, severe.
- Overall risk — "Low, medium or high": low, medium, high.
- The ICO: "any significant possibility of very serious harm may still be enough to qualify as a high risk", and severity is assessed "precautionary".
- Where a risk differs by age, the assessment is given by the age categories.

**The risks of std 2, Step 5, that the platform does not have by its design:**
- there is no communication between users, so there is no "online grooming" or "bullying or peer pressure" inside the service either;
- there is no advertising, no purchases inside a lesson and no tariffs for the child: there is no "economic exploitation" (О-14 — the owner's subscription);
- there are no timers, competitions, streaks or notifications: "compulsive use", "excessive screen time" and sleep are not affected. The child takes the next task themselves;
- there is no emotion inference and there are no decisions "without human review" about the child.

The other risks of std 2 — "access to harmful or inappropriate content", "misinformation", "undermining parental authority", "loss of autonomy or rights", "physical harm" — and the data risks stand in the table below.

## 10. The assessments of Connecticut, Colorado and Montana

This DPIA is also the assessment that these three states require of a controller that knows a user is a minor, under 18. In Connecticut it is a data protection assessment, and with profiling also an impact assessment; in Colorado and Montana, a data protection assessment at a heightened risk of harm to minors (sources 3.12). The platform knows the learner's age category but not their state, so it holds to these laws for every learner from the USA. Each law takes an assessment made for another law "if … reasonably similar in scope and effect" (CGS §42-529b(e); C.R.S. 6-1-1309.5(4); MCA 30-14-2819(4)). This DPIA does not rest on that clause: it answers each element itself. The texts were read on 2026-11-13, as the sources above say; the reading is Claude's, and the author accepts it.

**Who must, and from when.**
- **Connecticut.** CGS §42-529b binds "each controller that offers any online service, product or feature to consumers whom such controller has actual knowledge, or wilfully disregards, are minors". From 2026-07-01, a service that "engages in any profiling based on such consumers' personal data" also needs an impact assessment, §42-529b(b).
- **Colorado.** C.R.S. 6-1-1309.5(1) binds a controller that "on or after October 1, 2025" offers such a service, "if there is a heightened risk of harm to minors". The rules give the minimum content: Rule 8.02(A) covers a risk "to a Consumer or to a Minor", and Rule 8.04 lists the content. The profiling elements of Rule 9.06(F) apply to profiling under 6-1-1309(2)(a), one that "presents a reasonably foreseeable risk of" unfair treatment, injury, intrusion or "other substantial injury". Risk 7 is such a risk, however low, so the impact assessment of profiling below answers them too.
- **Montana.** MCA 30-14-2819(1) binds a controller from 2025-10-01, in the same words as Colorado. Under 30-14-2803(2) it applies with no threshold to "persons that conduct business in this state or deliver commercial products or services that are intentionally targeted to residents of this state". Whether a Texas operator that serves the whole country, by invitation and for free, does either is not verified, and it is taken to apply.

### 10.2. The impact assessment of profiling

CGS §42-529b(b) asks for an impact assessment when a service for minors "engages in any profiling". Profiling is "any form of automated processing performed on personal data to evaluate, analyze or predict personal aspects" (§42-515(31)). The learner model evaluates what a learner can do from their attempts, and Claude takes it for profiling, as version 10 did. The assessment holds the following, "to the extent reasonably known by or available to the controller, as applicable":

1. **Purpose, use cases, deployment, benefits.** The law asks for this statement only for profiling "for the purpose of making decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects", that is, a decision on "education enrollment or opportunity" among others (§42-515(15)). The learner model makes none: it gives no grade or certificate and decides no enrolment or access to education. The choice of a task is no such decision either: the learner may take any skill on the map. All the same: the purpose is to choose the next task — a new skill, consolidation, a repetition by the review date or a probe — within the success corridor. The use is the learner's own lessons and tasks. The model is deployed in `api`, by the libraries `learner-model` and `engine`. The benefit is tasks neither too easy nor too hard, and a return to a skill before it is forgotten.
2. **A heightened risk of harm, its nature and the steps against it.** This is risk 7: a label on a child, or a choice narrowed — "undue restriction on information". Colorado names a related harm as an example: "Intellectual privacy harms, such as the creation of negative inferences about an individual based on what an individual reads, learns, or debates" (Rule 8.04(A)(6)(b)). The model's inference stays within the platform. The learner and the adult of the account see it; nobody else does, it is not sold, and it is deleted with the profile. The steps are measure 7, and the residual risk is low.
3. **Inputs and outputs.** The inputs are the evidence of attempts — how each ended, after which hint, the traps found, each item's verdicts, the techniques, the requests for another explanation, whether the solution was seen — with each task's skills and difficulty, the skill graphs of the connected modules, and the grade a module asks for, which places the start, and the adult's correction of a module's level — easier or harder, and when. In the reading mode `single` an item's verdict rests on one reader's reading, and a misread one goes in as evidence like any other (risk 20). The work itself does not go in: no picture, no essay. Nor do the pseudonym or the country. The grade sets only the starting estimate, which the evidence then moves. The age category is an input of neither the model nor the engine, but `api` limits the modules, lessons and pool of tasks by it before the engine chooses, and the record of a choice keeps it. A task with a rubric gives no evidence (О-17), nor a task from paper. The outputs: for each skill, the estimate, its uncertainty, "mastered" with its band, the traps and the review date; a level per module; the skill map; and the engine's choice, with its reason code.
4. **Data used to customize the profiling.** No learner's data. The algorithms and their constants are `learner-model`'s: the Rasch model with a guessing floor, an Elo update and forgetting. A new variant is admitted only after it passes the learner simulator, by a criterion fixed before the first run.
5. **Transparency.** The policy names the learner model among the data, as "what the learner has shown they can do", and states that a program chooses the next task and that "you can see why it chose it" ("What we collect and why", "Your rights"). The learner sees the skill map. The adult sees each choice's reason code. The texts for a teenager say that the adult sees their progress (measure 16), and the learner's progress shows when an adult set a module's level; the texts name that correction with their next versions, before the first child (Д259).
6. **Monitoring and safeguards after deployment.** The learner chooses a skill on the map themselves, and ranks do not affect the model. The adult sees the reason codes and sets a module's tasks a band easier or harder in one step, and three failures in a row lower the level by a band. The model changes only through the learner simulator (item 4). A family's signal, and every task that changes the model's inputs, is a ground for review. The platform does not check on real learners whether the choice of tasks serves them: such a check would be a new processing, and this DPIA would be reviewed for it first.

**Colorado, Rule 9.06(F).** Its twelve elements, by number:
- 1 and 7, the data and the outputs: item 3;
- 2 and 4, the decision and why it belongs to the service: the choice of the next task, item 1;
- 3, the benefit of automated over manual processing: no person can follow every skill's estimate and review date for each learner, and the learner may still choose a skill by hand on the map;
- 5, the training data and the logic: item 4, with no learner's data;

- 10, how the system is checked "for fairness and disparate impact", and the results: by a criterion of the learner simulator — virtual learners of the same skill but different grades come to the same estimate and the same kind of task within a number of attempts written down before the first run. ### 10.3. Risks by the laws' kinds of harm

What each law calls a heightened risk of harm to minors, and the risks above that answer it:
- **unfair or deceptive treatment, unlawful disparate impact** (all three) — risks 7, 12, 19, 20, 21 and 23, risks 20 and 21 a false verdict: of one reader on the work, and of a model that judges a task from paper, and risk 23 the tutor's words or a hint that give the answer or point to no mistake;
- **financial injury** (all three) — none: there are no purchases and no tariffs for the child, and no economic exploitation;
- **physical injury** (all three) — risk 3: a disclosure of harm in an essay; **reputational injury** — risks 2 and 13;
- **unauthorized disclosure in a security breach** (Colorado, Montana) — risks 6 and 13; the order in a breach is the privacy document's, which `BREACH.md` of `infra` takes by Д249;
- **intrusion upon seclusion or private affairs** (all three) — risks 1, 2, 13, 15 and 24; there is no geolocation (std 10 of the ICO's code);
- **physical violence, material harassment, sexual abuse or exploitation** (Connecticut, §42-529(5)(D)–(F)) — there is no communication between users and nothing a child does is public; a disclosure in an essay is risk 3, with the operator's escalation path and §2258A (measure 3);
- **Colorado's further examples, Rule 8.04(A)(6):**
  - constitutional harms — none: nothing is published and nobody speaks to anyone;
  - intellectual privacy and discrimination — risk 7;
  - psychological harm — risk 4;
  - the private life of the home — risks 15 and 18.
