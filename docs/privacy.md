> Status: a draft for discussion, 2026-10-18.

## 1. Principles

- **Minimization.** We collect only what the platform cannot work without.
  - About a child: a pseudonym, an avatar from a set, the age category, the country, the interface language, the presentation style, the default solving mode and the adult's setting "ask before showing" the solution; the grade, if a module asks for it; interests, once the format gives a task styled to them (R98). There is no name, photo, date of birth, school or contact details.
  - The adult's contact details: only those without which sign-in and the subscription do not work.
- **Pseudonymization, not anonymity.** In the system a learner is a UUID and a pseudonym. Under GDPR such data stays personal (recital 26), and under COPPA a persistent identifier is personal information too. So we do not promise "full anonymity" (as privacy 1 canvas-api).
- **Control lies with the account owner.** An adult learner manages their own data; for a child, a parent or guardian does: consent, viewing, export, deletion (R06, R09).
- **Privacy by default**, by the UK Age Appropriate Design Code: no public profiles, no communication between learners, no geolocation, no advertising and no profiling for marketing purposes.
- **Strokes stay on the device.** Strokes with their time and pressure are stored on the learner's device. What goes for checking is a picture or a crop of the work; the server holds it only in the memory of the request and does not store it (R09; as R42 canvas-api).
- **The AI sees the work but does not know whose it is.** The platform passes the AI no name, no pseudonym, no identifiers and no data of the adult. But the picture may hold anything the learner wrote, a name included.
- **Honest retention periods.** Deletion is described with every place where data remains: backups, logs, other devices — they erase the data at their next request to `api` within an hour after the deletion, otherwise at the next sign-in on them, and a device that is not opened keeps it until the browser or the adult erases it (architecture 4.5) —, the sign-in service, the AI providers' logs and the record of consent (П-14). We give no promise that "nothing will remain anywhere". Data on the device may also be erased by the browser itself, without any action of the account owner: when space runs short, when the site's data is cleared, and Safari erases "all of a website's script-writable storage after seven days of Safari use without user interaction on the site" ([WebKit, Full Third-Party Cookie Blocking and More](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/), 2020-03-24, read by `app` 2026-11-02).

## 3. Age

### 3.1. Age category

| Category | What applies (top level) |
|---|---|
| under 13 | COPPA (USA): verifiable parental consent before data is collected. GDPR Art. 8 and UK GDPR: parental consent. UK AADC |
| from 13 to the country's age of digital consent | GDPR Art. 8: parental consent. UK AADC. Where the age of consent is 13, as in the United Kingdom, this category is empty |
| from the age of digital consent to 18 | consent — the learner themselves. UK AADC — up to 18. The AI providers' terms on minors (section 5.3) |
| 18 and over | the laws on adults' data; in the EU, GDPR |

- **The age of digital consent** under GDPR is 16. A country may lower it, but not below 13; in the United Kingdom it is 13.
- **By country**, the age of digital consent is in the table, [the part `consent-age/`](../consent-age/README.md): each country of the EU and the EEA, the United Kingdom and the USA, with the law and its provision, a quote, the primary source, the date it was read and the day from which the age applies (R134). In the USA there is no federal age above 13: COPPA requires parental consent under 13, and the state laws are examined by T107 (R131).
- **A country outside the table** has 18: there consent for a learner under 18 is given by a parent, and the category "from the age of digital consent to 18" is empty. The platform does not know a law that nobody has read, and an error in this direction is a parent's consent for a teenager who could have given their own (R134).
- **Art. 8 GDPR** applies when the lawful basis of the processing is consent. The basis (R116): an adult's own data — contract, Art. 6(1)(b); the data of a learner under 18 — consent, Art. 6(1)(a): below the age of digital consent, the parent's, under Art. 8, and under 13 also verifiable under COPPA; above it, the learner's own.
- **So the country is needed:** without it one cannot determine which category, for example, a 14-year-old belongs to.

### 3.2. What depends on the age category

- who owns the account and gives consent;
- which AI routes and which providers are available (section 5);
- voice input and photos from paper (section 6);
- which modules are available: a module may mark content as unsuitable for a category (concepts 4);
- what the learner is shown: under 18 — at work with a rubric and in the section "Help", open from any screen, — where to turn if something is wrong: to an adult they trust, and to the helplines of their country (R127).

### 3.3. How age is given

- The account owner gives the learner's age and the country (R06).
- The platform computes the age category and stores it, the country, the date when the age was given, and the country's age of digital consent by which the category was given — from 13 to 18, a property of the law, not of the learner (R134). It stores neither the date of birth nor the age in years.
- The age in years reaches the server in the application for child consent and lives only in the memory of the request, and for the owner profile the category is `adult` by the owner's own statement, without an age (R98).
- Once a year the category is updated: `app` asks the adult to give the age again when the profile is flagged — a year after the age was given. Until the update the previous category applies: it is stricter, because the learner has only grown older since.
- When the country changes, the age is given again: the age in years is not stored, and there is nothing to recompute the category from.
- Likewise when the country's age of digital consent has become higher than the one by which the category was given: a learner who gave consent themselves may have ended up below the new age, and before the next work the age is given again — also when the table recorded later a law passed earlier. A lowered age waits for the yearly update: the previous category is stricter (R134; [the part `consent-age/`](../consent-age/README.md), section 6).
- In all three cases the adult gives the age and the country by one method, `RestateAge`, and a new category that needs a different consent waits for it: the learner's own consent or a parent's consent with a new consent request code (R136).
- How to confirm that the account owner is an adult is П-6.
- **The presentation style** is not a legal concept: the length of phrases, voicing, the persona, ranks. It can be chosen separately (concepts 13).

## 5. AI routes

### 5.1. Platform AI

- The platform calls the AI under its own contracts with the providers.
- **Requirements for a provider:**
  - the provider does not train models on learners' data;
  - zero data retention is mandatory where the provider's terms require it: at OpenAI, for the personal data of children under 13 or under the age of digital consent (section 5.3). ### 5.2. Own key

- A learner or an account owner connects their own key of an AI provider.
- The calls go in the name of the key's owner: the provider sees their billing account and ties each request to it. The provider's terms are between the provider and the key's owner.
- The key is the key owner's secret. It lies on the device only if the key's owner so decided on this device, and otherwise only in the tab's memory while their profile is open; on the server only in the memory of the request, not in the database and not in the logs. There is no encryption in the browser: a key saved on the device can be taken by whoever holds the device, and `app` says so plainly. The key is not in the export (R84; R08 app).
- The key goes with each request of the owner profile that calls the AI, and with a request of a profile whose category is not `adult`, `api` does not accept it (R04 api; О-4).
- The providers' terms, by the author's decision, allow giving the key to `api` (R84). At OpenAI the contract is the Services Agreement, and in it §3.1 and §3.3(g) are on access to the account and on sharing keys; at Anthropic keys go under the Commercial Terms, which have no provision on sharing a key, and the help center describes a key in third-party tools (sources 2.3).
- The platform applies its own rules here too: what goes into a call — as in section 5.1; which models are admitted; to which age categories the route is open.
- The own key is closed for minors (R122; О-4).

## 6. Voice, photos and artifacts

- **The tutor's voicing** is for everyone: it is output, not data collection, as long as the text does not leave the device.
  - So `app` names the voice itself, only a speech synthesis voice of the device whose `localService` is true and whose name has no "Online" (Т-13; R07 app). Remote synthesis would receive the tutor's words bypassing `api`, without a contract with the platform, and feedback by a rubric quotes the learner's words (R70).
  - `localService` is known to the browser, not to the system. On Android and Linux, Chrome gives the page every system voice as local — `localService` is true for a network one too (R118) —, so whether the system synthesizes on the device the page cannot learn, and there voicing is turned on by the adult, having read why (sources 5.4). The browser gives the text to the synthesis engine by the adult's choice, as to their screen reader; this does not count as a disclosure by the platform — Claude's reading, the author's decision (R112; R118; П-22).
- **Voice input.**
  - Under COPPA a voiceprint is a biometric identifier, that is, personal information (16 CFR 312.2, "voiceprints").
  - The exception from parental consent is audio with a child's voice without other personal data. It must be collected only to respond to a specific request of the child, used for nothing else, disclosed to no one, and deleted immediately after the response (16 CFR 312.5(c)(9)).
  - Whether pronunciation assessment and passing the audio to an AI provider fall under the exception the author decides by analysis (R112; П-5). Until the decision, voice input is for adults only (О-6).
- **Photos from paper.**
  - A sheet may hold a name, a school, other people's texts.
  - So the task on the picture is marked by the learner or the adult, and the picture is cropped to it on the device, before the AI is called.
  - The task stays on the device and does not get into a shared pool (О-11).
- **Artifacts** — drawings, essays, recordings — belong to the learner. They are stored on the server only if the account owner turned this on for the profile, and a learner who gives consent themselves, for themselves, in their own profile (the second 2026-11-09 amendment to R113); by default, no (R113). The switch says that an essay may hold personal matters, about health and the family among them, and that nobody reads it: turning it on is explicit consent to this (R127). Its words are the text `storage-consent` of the part `legal/` (the 2026-11-09 amendment to R127).
- **An essay** is personal data of the learner and of those the learner names in it (R127). Before submitting a work that goes to the AI whole, the platform reminds the learner not to write names. It does not look for signs of harm in the essay, and to a learner under 18 it always shows where to turn if something is wrong.
